01 — Mission

Engineering validation first. Research contribution second.

The project deliberately separates the two questions. Stage 1 answers “does the hardware survive and function in flight?” Stage 2 answers “does the combined AI + flight-MCU payload produce new, publishable knowledge?”

Keeping them separate protects the research goal. A sensor or radio failure at 10–12 km is a cheap, recoverable lesson — not the loss of an expensive research flight. Stage 1 also builds real flight heritage for the team's own hardware, which is exactly the low-cost heritage argument the Stage 2 paper relies on.

Central research claim

No published paper or prototype combines all five elements — a true 1U CubeSat frame, a Teensy flight MCU, on-board Raspberry Pi AI inference, LoRa telemetry, and a 30–50 km balloon flight — in a single payload.

Each element has separate flight heritage. The combination does not exist in the reviewed literature.

03 — Architecture

Two functional blocks, one flight computer

The Stage 1 avionics are organised around an Electrical Power System that generates and conditions every voltage rail, and an I²C sensor array managed by a Teensy 4.1 through a PCA9548A multiplexer. UART peripherals and analog temperature sensors attach directly to the Teensy.

Power chain

Solar panels (4 × 6 V)
  ↓ TP4056 charger
  ↓ 4 × parallel LiPo (3200 mAh)
  ↓ 1S BMS + master switch
  ↓ XL6009 boost → 5 V rail
  ↓ dual AMS1117
  → 3.3 V digital · 3.3 V analog
Design note — split grounds

Two AMS1117 regulators create independent DIGITAL_GND and ANALOG_GND domains, joined at a single point through small 10 Ω resistors. This keeps switching noise off the analog sensor readings (TMP36, INA219) — which matters for clean temperature and current data.

Compute and data flow

I²C sensors
BME280, MPU6500, LIS3MDL and 2 × INA219 attach to a PCA9548A 8-channel multiplexer — each device isolated on its own channel to avoid address clashes and bus loading.
UART peripherals
NEO-6M GPS on Serial1 (pins 0/1). RYLR998 LoRa on Serial2 (pins 7/8).
Analog sensors
3 × TMP36 read on Teensy pins A0–A2 — internal, external and battery temperature.
Actuation
A single SG90 servo on PWM pin 14 deploys the parachute.
Storage
Teensy 4.1 built-in microSD slot logs all telemetry as CSV — no external SD module or SPI wiring required.
Stage 2 addition
Raspberry Pi + camera runs TFLite inference and talks to the Teensy over serial/I²C, so a Pi crash never takes down flight control or telemetry. Spare pins 2, 4–6, 9–13, 15–17, 20–21, 24–41 are reserved for the link.

Telemetry record

All data is written to the SD card as a single CSV, one row per sample.

timestamp_ms, pressure_hPa, temp_C, humidity_pct, mag_x_uT, mag_y_uT, mag_z_uT, accel_x, accel_y, accel_z, gps_lat, gps_lon, gps_alt_m, gps_sats, vbat_mV, vsys_mV
04 — Hardware

Every part, and what it does

The Stage 1 bill of materials, grouped by subsystem. Full pin-by-pin wiring and capacitor values live in the team's separate wiring document.

EPS

Power & battery

  • Solar panels (4 × 6 V) — generate power, feed the charger
  • 1N5819 Schottky ×4 — block reverse current into panels
  • LiPo 4 × 800 mAh — 3200 mAh parallel pack
  • TP4056 — safe solar battery charging
  • 1S BMS — over-charge / discharge / current protection
  • XL6009 — boosts 3.7–4.2 V to a steady 5 V rail
  • AMS1117-3.3 ×2 — separate logic and sensor 3.3 V supplies
  • INA219 ×2 — solar-side and system-side V/I monitoring
COMPUTE

Flight computer

  • Teensy 4.1 — the brain: reads sensors, runs flight logic, logs to SD, drives radio, GPS and servo
  • PCA9548A — splits one I²C bus into 8 channels
  • microSD Class 10 — CSV flight log in the Teensy's built-in slot
SENSING

Sensors

  • BME280 — pressure, temperature, humidity (I²C)
  • MPU6500 — 6-axis acceleration and rotation (I²C)
  • LIS3MDL — 3-axis magnetic field (I²C)
  • TMP36 ×3 — internal, external, battery temperature (analog)
NAV / COMMS

Navigation & telemetry

  • NEO-6M GPS — position, altitude and UTC over UART; also used to find the payload after landing
  • RYLR998 LoRa (433 MHz) — live telemetry via AT commands over UART
  • 433 MHz whip antenna — kept clear of the frame and the GPS antenna
RECOVERY

Recovery & structure

  • SG90 servo — releases the parachute at apogee or on command
  • 1U frame — 100 × 100 × 113.5 mm, survives ascent, burst and landing
  • Status LEDs ×3 — power, transmit, error
  • Reset button — manual Teensy restart
PLANNED

Flight-model additions

Kapton patch heaters for thermal control, to keep the electronics warm at the very cold temperatures of the higher Stage 2 flight. Not on the demo unit — to be added in a later revision.

05 — Research

The gap: nobody has combined all five

Before claiming the idea is new, we reviewed what others have built. Each project below did part of what we want to do — a Teensy-brained CubeSat, a solar-powered Teensy at altitude, a Pi sending images over LoRa, a Pi running edge AI. None did all of it together.

PRIOR WORK

CubeSat body, Teensy brain

Princeton TigerSats built an open-source 1U frame with a Teensy as main computer — but no camera, AI or LoRa. IIA Bangalore flew a 1U CubeSat to ~30 km in 2020 at −40 °C — but on an Arduino-class chip, with no camera or AI.

PRIOR WORK

Solar-powered Teensy at altitude

Bazán Palacios (2022) flew a Teensy on a small solar panel with INA219 monitoring. The panel gave more power up high than at ground level and the battery was never needed — good evidence our solar plan works. But power-only: no frame, camera or AI.

PRIOR WORK

Pi + camera over LoRa

Dave Akerman's “Pi In The Sky” reached ~42 km sending live pictures over LoRa — proving image downlink works, but with the Pi doing everything alone. Solid State Depot (2017 eclipse) used a Teensy only on the ground receiver, not as a flight computer.

PRIOR WORK

Pi running AI alone

TFLite on a Pi ran ~90% faster than the full framework. YOLO on the smallest Pi managed real-time detection — on-board inference beats downlinking everything when the link is weak, exactly our case at 30–50 km. But all tested on the ground or in orbit, never on a balloon with a separate flight computer.

Novelty statement

The combination of on-board Pi AI inference + an independent Teensy flight MCU + LoRa + a full sensor suite, inside a true 1U CubeSat frame, flown to 30–50 km, does not appear together in any published paper or prototype found in this review.

A credible basis for a novel student research paper.

06 — After Stage 2

Publish first, then four open paths

Everything depends on having a published result. As far as our review shows, no one has flown a 1U CubeSat carrying a Teensy flight computer and a Raspberry Pi running on-board AI to 30–50 km. Publishing puts our name on it first — after that, four paths are open, and we can follow more than one at a time.

  • Right away

    Publish the research

    Write up the build and flight data with Dr. Sen's review, submit to a student research conference, post a dated preprint, and open the wiring notes, code, AI model and flight data so others can repeat it.

  • Right away · Path D

    Run paid workshops

    A “What is a CubeSat?” session plus a hands-on kit, starting inside our own college through the club and Music Lab, then offered to nearby colleges and schools. Funds the next flight without depending only on club money.

  • Near term · Path C

    Enter competitions

    CanSat India / Model Rocketry Student Competition — run by IN-SPACe, ISRO and the Astronautical Society of India; its 2025 final at Kushinagar drew 67 teams and 500+ students. Adapt the existing CubeSat to the rules rather than starting from scratch.

  • 6–18 months · Path A

    Team up with a university lab

    With the paper in hand, ask Dr. Sen for introductions to IIT, IIST or IIA Bangalore groups. Offer to collaborate on the next version — attitude control, for example — with credit agreed early.

  • Long term · Path B

    Apply through IN-SPACe / ISRO

    The ISRO Student Satellite Programme gives approved college teams mentoring and even a launch. By early 2026 IN-SPACe had approved 17 student satellites and payloads, 11 already launched. Our balloon flights become the proof the design works.

Where this leads

The long-term goal is to turn this design into a real 1U CubeSat launched into orbit — using balloon flights as low-cost proof that it works, flying several times for solid results, and adding attitude control as the bridge from a balloon demo to a true satellite.
